# Research: Art Projects with Rotary Phones

**Research Date:** April 19, 2026
**Topic:** Contemporary and maker art projects that repurpose the rotary telephone

---

## Overview

The rotary dial telephone — obsolete as a communication device since the 1980s — has
become one of the most durable icons in contemporary art and maker culture. Its tactile
weight, mechanical dial, bakelite shell, and disconnected receiver make it a ready-made
vessel for themes of memory, grief, absence, nostalgia, surveillance, and reclaimed
attention. This document surveys the most significant rotary-phone art projects across
four categories: memorial and conceptual installations, public art, interactive museum
exhibits, and maker/DIY repurposing.

---

## 1. Memorial and Conceptual Installations

### The Wind Phone (風の電話 / *Kaze no Denwa*) — Itaru Sasaki, 2010
The single most influential rotary-phone artwork of the 21st century. Garden designer
Itaru Sasaki installed a white, glass-paned booth containing a disconnected black rotary
phone in his garden in Ōtsuchi, Iwate Prefecture, Japan, to "talk" to his cousin who
had died of cancer. After the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami killed more than
15,000 people, Sasaki opened the booth to the public. Over 30,000 grieving visitors
have used it to hold one-way conversations with lost loved ones. Sasaki said: *"Because
my thoughts couldn't be relayed over a regular phone line, I wanted them to be carried
on the wind."* Dozens of replica "wind phones" now exist worldwide — a rare example of
a single rotary-phone artwork spawning a global participatory movement.

### Once Upon a Place — Aman Mojadidi, 2017
For Times Square Arts, Afghan-American artist Aman Mojadidi rewired three decommissioned
NYC phone booths so that lifting the receiver played oral histories he had recorded
with immigrants to New York City. The piece turned obsolete civic infrastructure into a
site-specific archive, activating the idea that phone booths are already haunted by the
calls once made inside them.

### Submerged Phone Booth — Banksy, 2006
Though it depicts a British payphone rather than a rotary desk set, Banksy's sculpture
of a bent, "wounded" red phone box with a pickaxe buried in its side belongs to the
same conceptual family: a dead communication object mourning the death of a mode of
communication.

### Telepoem Booths — Elizabeth Hellstern
Hellstern refits decommissioned booths with rotary-style directories that let visitors
"dial" a poem by entering a poet's phone-number-coded listing. Installed across the US,
the project treats the rotary interface as a slow, deliberate ritual that re-frames how
we access language.

## 2. Public Art

### "Phone Banksy" (Phone Wall) — Dave Gatzmer, Minnesota
Twin Cities artist Dave Gatzmer has mounted more than 200 telephones — including many
rotary models — on a freeway noise barrier in St. Louis Park. What started as five
phones has become a rolling, accidental museum of telephony visible from the highway.
Gatzmer preps each unit by gluing receivers in place and bolting metal straps to the
back for mounting. The piece is part folk art, part memorial to a vanished object.

### Out of Disorder — Takahiro Iwasaki
Japanese sculptor Takahiro Iwasaki builds miniature telephone towers carved from wood
and wire, drawing a direct line between traditional craft and the fragility of modern
communication infrastructure. The series sits in the adjacent space of "rotary phone
art" — it treats the whole analog-phone system as a subject, not just the handset.

### Telephone Masts / voice installations — Jim Campbell
Jim Campbell's pieces route live telephone audio into LED pixel fields, letting the
viewer speak into a receiver and watch their voice become light. Often shown with
rotary and vintage receivers to emphasize that the "voice" is the medium, not the
device.

## 3. Interactive Museum Exhibits

A standard vocabulary has emerged around the rotary phone as a museum interface:

- **Pick-up-the-receiver oral history stations.** A dimly lit period-room exhibit
  centers a black rotary phone; lifting the heavy handset plays a brief line-crackle
  followed by an oral history — a Dust Bowl refugee, a factory worker, a rationing-era
  homemaker. The weight and click of a real rotary handset does pedagogical work that
  a touchscreen cannot.
- **Dial-a-story.** Visitors actually dial a digit (1–9) to choose which narrator
  they hear; the mechanical dial-return becomes part of the storytelling rhythm.
- **Ambient sound design.** Modern builds add subtle handset vibration to simulate
  ringing, with directional speakers spreading the "overheard call" into the gallery
  so passers-by catch fragments.

### Art By Telephone — MCA Chicago, 1969
A proto-conceptual landmark worth flagging: the Museum of Contemporary Art Chicago's
1969 exhibition *Art By Telephone* asked 37 artists (including Joseph Beuys, John
Baldessari, and Richard Artschwager) to *phone in* their instructions for works the
museum would then fabricate. The rotary phone was the medium of authorship — an early
argument that a work of art could be transmitted, not shipped.

## 4. Maker and DIY Repurposing

A rich parallel tradition treats the rotary phone as a hackable enclosure. These
projects sit between craft, art, and engineering.

### Electronics-focused builds
- **Arduino Rotary Phone (Make: Magazine).** A Western Electric 500 D gutted and
  rebuilt around an Arduino and audio shield. Dialing triggers voice recording, plays
  back previous callers, or streams live distorted audio to the earpiece — turning
  the phone into a sound-art instrument.
- **GSM-enabled rotary phone.** Makers have paired an Arduino Pro Mini with a SIM900
  GSM shield so a mid-century rotary handset can actually place cellular calls, send
  DTMF tones, and store speed dials. The dial pulses are decoded in software.
- **Ringer challenge.** A recurring engineering obstacle: the original electromechanical
  bell ringer needs ~90 VAC to fire — far beyond what a microcontroller supplies — so
  builders add boost converters, H-bridges, or replace the ringer with a relay-driven
  solenoid.
- **Raspberry Pi "radiooooo" phone (Caroline Buttet).** A Pi inside a rotary phone
  talks to radiooooo.com; dialing a country on a map plays music from that country and
  decade through the earpiece. A travel-and-time machine disguised as a 1950s object.
- **Google AIY rotary phone.** A Raspberry Pi 3 + AIY Voice Kit build with a dial
  tone, hang-up click, and a voice "operator" — a conversational-AI interface wearing
  analog clothing.
- **Rotary jukebox.** Pi-based builds where each number on the dial maps to a music
  library or streaming playlist, with the original receiver as the speaker.

### Object-focused builds
- **Lamps.** The receiver is reworked into a light arm; the base holds the socket.
- **Planters.** The ringer well becomes a succulent bed.
- **Bookends, clocks, and doorbells.** The dial face is often swapped for a clock
  face; the ringer is rewired to trigger from a wireless doorbell module.
- **Wall assemblage.** Dozens of rotary phones Velcroed or strapped onto a board as
  gallery-ready collage.

## Recurring Themes

Across all four categories, a few motifs keep surfacing:

1. **Grief and the one-way call.** The disconnected receiver is a near-perfect
   metaphor for speaking to someone who cannot answer — the Wind Phone is its purest
   expression.
2. **Slowed attention.** The mechanical dial takes real time to return. Artists use
   this friction deliberately, against the frictionless swipe of smartphones.
3. **The handset as a private acoustic space.** Pressing a heavy receiver to one ear
   is intimate in a way headphones are not; museums and installation artists exploit
   this for oral history, poetry, and confessional audio.
4. **The phone as a haunted object.** Many pieces lean on the idea that every phone
   already contains the ghost of every call made through it.
5. **Analog shell, digital guts.** Maker culture consistently keeps the bakelite
   body intact while replacing the internals — a visual argument that form and
   interaction matter more than the underlying signal.

---

## Ideas for Future Projects

A short list of under-explored directions:

- **Collective wind phone.** A networked rotary phone that records spoken messages and
  plays them, anonymized and shuffled, to the next caller — extending Sasaki's idea
  into an asynchronous global chorus.
- **Dial-a-dataset.** A rotary phone that reads aloud entries from a public dataset
  (weather archive, congressional record, climate data) chosen by the dialed number.
- **AI operator.** A Claude- or LLM-driven "operator" at the other end of the line,
  with the phone's slow dial pacing the conversation and discouraging doomscrolling.
- **Phone as attention timer.** A rotary handset you must physically lift (and keep
  lifted) to unlock focus-mode on your computer.
- **Urban wind-phone network.** Decommissioned booths re-keyed as neighborhood grief
  stations with shared, moderated audio.
